<TLDR>

A great product that nobody hears about is a hobby. Cold-calling schools for Oxygen LMS was uncomfortable, but every call gave me information no analytics dashboard could. Here is the process I used and what it taught me.

</TLDR>

After the POS, I promised myself the next product would start with customers, not code. For Oxygen LMS, an operating system for schools, that meant doing the thing most engineers avoid: picking up the phone.

## Why the phone

Schools in Pakistan don’t discover software through Product Hunt or Google Ads. Decisions are made by a principal or an owner who is busy, cautious and gets pitched by a lot of people. A landing page doesn’t reach them. A short, respectful conversation sometimes does.

<Callout type="note">

“Cold calling” here means calling schools I had no relationship with, using publicly listed numbers, and asking for ten minutes, not a sale.

</Callout>

## The process

<Timeline>
  <Event date="Week 1" title="Build a list">
    A spreadsheet of private schools in one city: name, phone, size, fee range, who decides.
  </Event>
  <Event date="Week 1–2" title="Write a 20-second opener">
    Who I am, the one problem I’m solving, and a small ask. No feature list.
  </Event>
  <Event date="Week 2–4" title="Call every afternoon">
    A fixed block every day. Log every call in the sheet: outcome, objection, next step.
  </Event>
  <Event date="Week 4" title="Visit the warm ones">
    Sit in the office, watch how attendance and fees actually work, and show a rough demo.
  </Event>
</Timeline>

### The opener

The first version of my script was a feature list. It failed. The version that worked was short and about *their* problem:

> “Assalam o Alaikum, I’m Gulzaib, a software engineer in Islamabad. I’m building a simple system for fee collection and attendance for schools like yours, and I’m speaking to principals before I finish it. Could I ask you two quick questions about how you handle fees today?”

Asking for advice instead of a sale changes the tone of the whole call. People like being asked about their work.

## What the calls taught me

Most calls go nowhere. That’s fine. The value is in the pattern across dozens of them.

- **The pain wasn’t where I expected.** I thought schools wanted smart timetables. What came up again and again was fee collection and parent communication ([what schools actually pay for](/blog/fee-vouchers-not-dashboards)).
- **The user isn’t the buyer.** Teachers use the system, the owner pays for it, and the admin clerk can quietly kill it. You need all three.
- **Simplicity is a feature.** “My staff can’t use complicated software” was one of the most common objections. More on that in the next post.
- **Trust is local.** “Which other schools use it?” came up on almost every positive call. Your first three customers are your marketing.

<PullQuote>

Every rejected call is a free lesson in positioning. A landing page with no traffic teaches you nothing.

</PullQuote>

## The founder’s math

Distribution is a numbers game, so treat it like one. The exact numbers vary by market. The shape of the funnel doesn’t.

| Stage | What it means | What to measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dial | You called | Calls per day |
| Connect | You reached the decision maker | Connect rate |
| Conversation | They gave you two minutes | Conversations per week |
| Visit or demo | They agreed to meet | Demo rate |
| Pilot | They agreed to try it | Pilots, and why the others said no |

<Callout type="tip" title="Log the objections, not just the outcomes">

A column for *“why not?”* is the most valuable column in the sheet. After a few weeks it becomes your roadmap and your FAQ.

</Callout>

## For engineers who hate selling

I get it. I’m an engineer too. Three things made it bearable:

1. **Time-box it.** One hour a day, then back to code. You don’t need to become a salesperson. You just need to learn.
2. **Use a script, then drop it.** The script gets you through the first ten calls. After that, you’ll know what to say.
3. **Remember you’re helping.** If the product genuinely solves their problem, telling them about it is a service.

<Takeaways>

- Your customers’ buying habits decide your distribution channel, not your preferences.
- Ask for advice, not a sale.
- Log every objection. That’s your roadmap.
- Your first customers are your best marketing. Make them succeed.

</Takeaways>
