<TLDR>

Technical founders rarely fail because they cannot build. They fail because building gives them a convincing way to postpone the more uncomfortable work of discovering whether anyone urgently wants what they are building.

The code improves every week. The architecture becomes cleaner. More features are completed. Yet the number of serious customer conversations remains close to zero.

This is not laziness. It is not necessarily a lack of ambition or discipline. It is a predictable consequence of applying an engineering mindset to a problem that cannot be solved through engineering alone.

Building produces visible progress under your control. Distribution exposes you to uncertainty, rejection, pricing pressure, organizational politics, and evidence that your original assumptions may be wrong.

So when a technical founder must choose between polishing the product and contacting another potential customer, the product often wins.

That preference feels productive.

It can also destroy the company.

</TLDR>

## The product is advancing, but the business is standing still

Consider a familiar early-stage startup.

A founder spends six months building an AI platform for educational institutions. The system includes:

- Role-based access control
- AI-assisted content generation
- Analytics dashboards
- Subscription management
- Multi-tenant architecture
- Automated notifications
- Audit trails
- Integrations with third-party services

The application is technically impressive. It handles edge cases, looks credible in demonstrations, and could theoretically serve thousands of institutions.

But important questions remain unanswered:

- Which type of institution has the strongest need?
- Who experiences the problem directly?
- Who controls the budget?
- How are institutions solving the problem today?
- What event makes them look for a new solution?
- Which requirements are necessary for adoption?
- How long does procurement take?
- Is implementation part of the purchase?
- Will customers pay enough to support the business?
- Can the founder repeatedly reach similar customers?

The product may be 80% complete as software while the business remains 10% understood.

This imbalance is common because product progress is easier to observe. A founder can count features, deployments, commits, screens, integrations, and completed tickets.

Commercial progress is more ambiguous.

A conversation may reveal that the intended buyer has no budget. A demonstration may end without a clear next step. A prospect may compliment the product and never respond again. A pilot may expose a requirement that invalidates months of technical decisions.

Product development usually creates the feeling of forward motion.

Market contact may force the founder to move backward before moving forward.

That is exactly why it matters.

## Technical founders are trained to solve the wrong kind of uncertainty

Software engineering and early-stage distribution both involve uncertainty, but they involve different kinds of uncertainty.

### Technical uncertainty

Technical uncertainty includes questions such as:

- Can the system process the required volume?
- Which database structure fits the access patterns?
- How should retries and failures be handled?
- Can the model achieve acceptable accuracy?
- How should user permissions be enforced?
- Can the application work with unreliable connectivity?
- How should data be synchronized?
- Which architecture will support future integrations?

These questions can often be resolved through research, experimentation, measurement, and implementation.

The founder controls much of the environment.

### Market uncertainty

Market uncertainty includes different questions:

- Does this problem matter enough to solve now?
- Who cares about it most?
- Is that person able to buy?
- Does the organization already have a budget?
- What alternative must the product displace?
- What risk does the buyer associate with changing systems?
- What promise will earn a conversation?
- What evidence will create trust?
- Which channel can repeatedly reach these buyers?
- Will customers continue paying after the initial excitement fades?

These answers do not exist inside the codebase.

They exist in customer behavior, budgets, internal processes, incentives, existing relationships, and purchasing decisions.

A founder cannot reason their way to these answers from first principles alone. They can form hypotheses, but those hypotheses must eventually face the market.

This creates a psychological and operational conflict.

Technical founders are accustomed to being rewarded for solving difficult problems independently. Early distribution requires them to admit that some of the most important information belongs to other people.

## Building feels safer because it preserves control

When you write software, the feedback loop is comparatively clean:

1. Write code
2. Run tests
3. Observe failure
4. Fix issue
5. Deploy improvement

Distribution has a more difficult feedback loop:

1. Choose a market
2. Find a prospect
3. Contact them
4. Receive no response
5. Change the message
6. Get a meeting
7. Discover the problem is weak
8. Choose another segment
9. Repeat

The second loop creates more emotional resistance because failure is less contained.

A failed test says something about the code.

An unanswered message can feel like it says something about the founder, the idea, or the company.

A rejected proposal may challenge:

- The product
- The pricing
- The positioning
- The target market
- The founder’s credibility
- The size of the opportunity
- The previous six months of work

It is therefore easier to return to an environment where progress feels objective.

You can improve loading speed.

You can refactor the service layer.

You can add another dashboard.

You can redesign onboarding.

You can migrate to a more fashionable infrastructure.

These activities may be legitimate. But they can also become a sophisticated form of avoidance.

The work is real. The progress is not always commercially meaningful.

## The four risks technical founders confuse

Early-stage founders often treat the startup as one large product-development problem. In reality, they face at least four different risks.

### Technical risk

Can the product be built reliably?

Examples:

- The AI model may not achieve sufficient accuracy.
- Real-time synchronization may fail under unreliable connectivity.
- Integration with legacy software may be difficult.
- Infrastructure costs may make the product uneconomical.
- Sensitive data may require stronger security controls.

Technical founders are usually comfortable identifying and reducing this risk.

### Problem risk

Is the problem painful, frequent, and important enough?

A customer may admit that a workflow is inefficient without caring enough to change it.

For example, a school might spend several days preparing examination materials. That sounds like a meaningful problem. But the school may consider it a normal seasonal burden rather than something worth purchasing software to solve.

The existence of inefficiency does not prove demand.

### Market risk

Is there a reachable group of customers with similar needs and purchasing behavior?

Ten organizations may want the product for ten different reasons. One may require offline access. Another may demand extensive customization. A third may want only reporting. A fourth may expect implementation and training to be included.

You may have individual interest without having a coherent market.

A viable initial market usually has enough similarity across:

- Pain
- Workflow
- Buyer
- Budget
- Urgency
- Required outcome
- Adoption process

### Distribution risk

Can you repeatedly reach, convince, and convert these customers at a sustainable cost?

A product may solve a real problem in a valuable market and still fail because the founder cannot establish a dependable path to customers.

This happens when:

- Buyers are difficult to identify.
- Decision-makers ignore cold outreach.
- Sales depend entirely on the founder’s personal network.
- Procurement takes nine months.
- Customers require expensive on-site implementation.
- Partnerships produce introductions but few qualified opportunities.
- Content attracts developers rather than buyers.
- Paid acquisition costs more than the expected customer value.
- Every deal requires a different message and product configuration.

Building more features primarily reduces technical risk.

It does not automatically reduce problem, market, or distribution risk.

That distinction changes how a founder should allocate time.

## Why “build first, market later” is so dangerous

The build-first model usually rests on a reasonable-sounding belief:

Once the product is good enough, it will be easier to sell.

Sometimes that is true. Certain products need a credible demonstration before customers can understand their value.

But “good enough to demonstrate” and “complete enough to scale” are very different thresholds.

A founder may need:

- A clickable prototype
- One working workflow
- A manually supported service
- A limited pilot
- A small internal tool
- A proof of concept using customer data

They rarely need the complete vision before beginning serious customer discovery.

The longer customer contact is delayed, the more assumptions become embedded in the product.

Those assumptions affect:

- Database structure
- Permissions
- User roles
- Workflows
- Integrations
- Pricing logic
- Hosting decisions
- Mobile and offline behavior
- Onboarding
- Reporting
- Support requirements

If the assumptions are wrong, the founder is not merely changing the marketing message. They may need to restructure the product.

<PullQuote>

A technically correct product can encode a false market model

</PullQuote>

Suppose you build an examination system under these assumptions:

- Teachers will create examinations directly in the platform.
- Institutions have reliable internet access.
- Every teacher has an individual account.
- Administrators will purchase the software centrally.
- Institutions prefer standardized workflows.
- Subscription payments will be made monthly.
- Existing student data can be imported in a consistent format.

The software may work perfectly according to those assumptions.

Real institutions may operate differently:

- Teachers may submit questions through Word documents or WhatsApp.
- Computer labs may have intermittent connectivity.
- A small examination team may operate the entire workflow.
- The owner may make the buying decision personally.
- Each institution may insist on its existing paper format.
- Customers may prefer annual invoices or delayed payments.
- Student records may be fragmented across spreadsheets.

The problem is not poor engineering.

The system was engineered around an imagined operating environment.

This is how technically strong products fail in real markets.

## More features do not automatically create more demand

When prospects do not buy, founders often conclude that the product is missing something.

The response is predictable:

- Add reporting
- Add automation
- Add integrations
- Add mobile support
- Add AI
- Add user roles
- Add customization
- Add another pricing tier

This creates a dangerous loop:

<Flow steps={["Low customer interest", "Assume product is incomplete", "Build more features", "Delay customer contact", "Receive little new evidence", "Continue building"]} />

The reasoning is attractive because it gives the founder an actionable task. But low conversion can have many causes unrelated to missing features.

The actual problem may be:

- The target segment is too broad.
- The pain is not urgent.
- The founder is speaking to users rather than buyers.
- The message is too technical.
- The buyer does not trust a new company.
- There is no allocated budget.
- The switching cost is too high.
- The customer needs implementation, not just software.
- The purchase is triggered only during a specific season.
- The existing workaround is cheap and familiar.
- The product requires too much behavioral change.
- The founder has selected the wrong distribution channel.

A new feature solves only one class of problem: a meaningful capability is missing from an otherwise viable purchase.

Before building it, ask:

- How many qualified prospects requested it?
- Did they identify it independently?
- Is it blocking a real deal or improving a hypothetical one?
- Is the request coming from the user, champion, or budget owner?
- Would the customer sign a conditional agreement if it were delivered?
- Does the feature serve the intended market or pull the product toward another segment?
- Can the desired outcome be delivered manually during a pilot?

A feature request attached to a commitment is evidence.

A feature request attached to a compliment is an opinion.

## Compliments are one of the most dangerous forms of false progress

Technical founders often receive encouraging feedback:

- “This is a great idea.”
- “Schools really need this.”
- “AI is the future.”
- “Let us know when it is ready.”
- “This could become very big.”
- “I know several people who might be interested.”

These statements feel validating, especially after months of isolated development.

But they cost the speaker nothing.

A prospect can admire a product without intending to:

- Introduce the decision-maker
- Share internal data
- Schedule another meeting
- Test the product
- Change an existing workflow
- Allocate staff time
- Obtain approval
- Sign a pilot
- Pay

Early-stage founders need to distinguish social support from commercial evidence.

### A practical evidence ladder

From weakest to strongest:

1. The person likes the idea.
2. The person agrees that the problem exists.
3. The person describes experiencing the problem recently.
4. The person has attempted to solve it.
5. The person is currently searching for a solution.
6. The person introduces other stakeholders.
7. The organization shares its workflow or data.
8. The team commits time to a pilot.
9. The buyer signs an agreement.
10. The customer pays.
11. The customer uses the product repeatedly.
12. The customer renews, expands, or refers another buyer.

The lower levels may justify another conversation.

They do not justify another six months of engineering.

## Technical founders often sell the system instead of the outcome

A technical founder naturally sees the product through its implementation.

They talk about:

- Models
- APIs
- Architecture
- Automation
- Microservices
- Dashboards
- Integrations
- Infrastructure
- Algorithms
- Technical differentiation

The customer sees the product through a different lens.

They care about:

- Time saved
- Revenue gained
- Costs reduced
- Mistakes prevented
- Risk controlled
- Compliance improved
- Work simplified
- Visibility increased
- Customers retained
- Decisions accelerated

Consider these two descriptions.

System-focused message:

> We built an AI-powered, multi-tenant examination platform with automated question generation, role-based workflows, analytics, audit logs, and intelligent assessment capabilities.

Outcome-focused message:

> We help examination teams reduce the time required to create, review, and organize assessments while keeping final academic approval with their faculty.

The first description may impress another engineer.

The second gives an examination administrator a reason to continue the conversation.

This does not mean hiding the technology. Technical credibility matters, especially when buyers care about security, reliability, integrations, or AI accuracy.

But technology should support the value proposition rather than replace it.

The buyer should understand the outcome before being asked to appreciate the machinery.

## Product quality and distribution cannot compensate for each other

The argument for early distribution is sometimes misinterpreted as:

Product quality does not matter. Just sell aggressively.

That is equally dangerous.

Distribution can bring people to the product. It cannot force them to remain.

A weak product may generate:

- Demonstrations without adoption
- Trials without activation
- Pilots without conversion
- Customers without renewal
- Attention without advocacy

Likewise, a strong product without distribution may generate:

- Positive feedback from a small network
- Occasional referrals
- A few loyal users
- No predictable pipeline
- No dependable revenue
- No path to scale

Product and distribution solve different problems.

The product must deliver value.

Distribution must repeatedly connect that value with the right market.

Before product-market fit, the two should develop together:

This loop is slower emotionally but faster economically.

It prevents the founder from scaling development around untested assumptions.

## Early distribution is part of product development

Distribution before product-market fit is not conventional marketing.

You are not trying to maximize traffic, followers, impressions, or lead volume.

You are trying to discover:

- Which customer should be served first
- Which problem creates action
- Which result deserves payment
- Which message earns attention
- Which objections appear repeatedly
- Which requirements block adoption
- Which buyer can authorize the decision
- Which sales process matches the market
- Which channel produces qualified conversations
- Which customers are likely to remain

Every distribution activity should reduce uncertainty.

Founder-led sales reveals:

- How customers describe the problem
- Who participates in the purchase
- Which objections block progress
- What outcomes buyers value
- What prospects are willing to pay

Outbound outreach reveals:

- Whether the target market is identifiable
- Whether the message earns responses
- Which job titles experience the problem
- Which trigger events create urgency
- Whether similar accounts react similarly

Content reveals:

- Which problems attract the right audience
- Which ideas generate serious replies
- What language resonates
- Whether expertise can create trust
- Which topics lead to customer conversations

Communities reveal:

- What customers discuss without prompting
- Which problems recur
- What terminology they use
- Which existing tools they dislike
- Who influences purchasing opinions

Partnerships reveal:

- Whether trusted intermediaries see customer demand
- Whether your product complements an existing service
- Whether another organization can repeatedly introduce buyers
- Whether the economic incentives are strong enough to sustain cooperation

These are not merely promotional channels.

They are market-sensing mechanisms.

## The founder cannot delegate this learning too early

A common technical-founder plan is:

Finish the product.
Hire a salesperson.
Ask the salesperson to find customers.

This sequence often fails because the salesperson inherits unresolved questions:

- Who is the ideal customer?
- What pain should lead the conversation?
- Which role should be contacted?
- What qualifies a serious opportunity?
- What result should be promised?
- Which objections matter?
- How should the product be demonstrated?
- How long is the sales cycle?
- What pricing model fits the buyer?
- Why do customers choose this product over doing nothing?

A good salesperson can execute and improve an existing motion.

They cannot reliably manufacture product positioning, market selection, pricing, qualification, and sales strategy from a blank page while the founder remains separated from customers.

If the product has not found a repeatable motion, hiring sales may delegate confusion rather than generate revenue.

Before making the first dedicated sales hire, the founder should ideally be able to explain:

- The characteristics of a qualified account
- The likely buyer and champion
- The initial outreach message
- The structure of a discovery call
- The common objections
- The product demonstration sequence
- The pilot or purchase offer
- The approximate sales cycle
- The reasons deals are won or lost
- The onboarding path to first value

The founder does not need perfect answers.

They need enough direct experience to recognize patterns.

## Distribution avoidance often looks like responsible engineering

The difficulty is that avoidance rarely looks irresponsible.

It appears as professional work:

- Improving test coverage
- Refactoring old modules
- Redesigning the interface
- Adding logging
- Implementing advanced permissions
- Upgrading infrastructure
- Preparing for scale
- Building an internal admin panel
- Adding more integrations
- Automating a process that has never been performed manually

Each task can be defended individually.

The problem appears when you examine the portfolio of work.

If the company has spent 500 hours developing and five hours speaking with buyers, the allocation itself is evidence.

The founder is reducing the risk they understand while ignoring the risks most likely to kill the company.

### A useful question

Before starting any major development task, ask:

What customer evidence makes this the most important problem to solve now?

Strong answers include:

- Three active pilot customers are blocked by it.
- It prevents users from reaching the core outcome.
- It appears in eight of the last ten qualified conversations.
- A buyer has agreed to proceed once it is available.
- Usage data shows that customers repeatedly fail at this step.
- It is a non-negotiable security or regulatory requirement.
- It is necessary to deliver the agreed pilot outcome.

Weak answers include:

- Competitors have it.
- It would make the product look complete.
- Customers may want it eventually.
- It will be harder to add later.
- It is technically interesting.
- It may help us scale.
- Someone mentioned it in a meeting.

The weak answers are not always wrong. They are simply insufficient on their own.

## The cost of premature architecture

Technical founders often build for the company they hope to have rather than the evidence they currently possess.

They prepare for:

- Millions of users
- Multiple countries
- Complex billing
- Dozens of permission combinations
- White-label deployment
- A large integration ecosystem
- Enterprise-scale reporting
- Automated onboarding
- Sophisticated recommendation systems

This can produce powerful software.

It can also create several costs.

1. Slower learning

Every additional system takes time away from customer contact and experimentation.

2. Greater resistance to change

The more you build around a market assumption, the harder it becomes to abandon it.

3. Emotional attachment

A large investment makes founders defend the existing direction even when customer evidence weakens.

4. Increased maintenance

Infrastructure, integrations, and abstractions create ongoing operational work before revenue exists to support it.

5. False confidence

A polished product can make an uncertain business appear more validated than it is.

Good early architecture should preserve change, not predict the entire company.

The goal is not to build carelessly. It is to match engineering investment with commercial evidence.

## What to validate before building more

You do not need complete certainty before development. Startups exist because certainty is impossible.

But before expanding the product significantly, you should be able to state your current hypotheses clearly.

1. Customer hypothesis: we believe this specific type of organization experiences the problem most intensely.

Example: private schools with 500 to 2,000 students that coordinate examinations through spreadsheets, Word documents, and WhatsApp.

2. Problem hypothesis: we believe this recurring workflow creates a meaningful operational or financial cost.

Example: examination teams lose several working days coordinating question collection, review, formatting, and approval during each examination cycle.

3. Buyer hypothesis: we believe this role can authorize or strongly influence the purchase.

Example: the school owner, principal, director of academics, or examination controller.

4. Trigger hypothesis: we believe this event makes the problem urgent.

Example: a coming examination cycle, expansion into new grades, an increase in student volume, or a previous examination error.

5. Value hypothesis: we believe the customer will pay to achieve this measurable outcome.

Example: reduce preparation time by 40% while retaining faculty review and approval.

6. Distribution hypothesis: we believe we can repeatedly reach these buyers through this channel.

Example: direct founder outreach supported by introductions from school consultants and education networks.

7. Adoption hypothesis: we believe the product can fit the customer’s existing environment without unacceptable change.

Example: the workflow can import existing Word or spreadsheet data, operate under inconsistent connectivity, and be managed by a small central examination team.

These are not facts.

They are testable statements.

That is useful because a testable hypothesis can be supported, rejected, or refined. A vague vision can survive indefinitely without producing knowledge.

## A distribution readiness scorecard

Use the following scorecard to assess whether you are building with the market or in isolation.

Score each statement from 0 to 3:

- 0: No evidence
- 1: Weak or anecdotal evidence
- 2: Repeated evidence
- 3: Strong behavioral or commercial evidence

| Category | Statement | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer clarity | We can define our initial customer segment narrowly. | 0–3 |
| Customer clarity | We know which role experiences the problem. | 0–3 |
| Customer clarity | We know who influences the purchase. | 0–3 |
| Customer clarity | We know who controls the budget. | 0–3 |
| Problem evidence | At least five target customers have described the problem from recent experience. | 0–3 |
| Problem evidence | We understand the customer’s current workflow. | 0–3 |
| Problem evidence | We know what the existing process costs in time, money, risk, or lost opportunity. | 0–3 |
| Problem evidence | Customers have previously attempted to solve the problem. | 0–3 |
| Commercial evidence | Qualified prospects have agreed to demonstrations or follow-up meetings. | 0–3 |
| Commercial evidence | Prospects have shared data, workflows, or internal requirements. | 0–3 |
| Commercial evidence | At least one customer has made a meaningful pilot commitment. | 0–3 |
| Commercial evidence | At least one customer has paid or agreed to defined commercial terms. | 0–3 |
| Distribution evidence | We can identify target accounts consistently. | 0–3 |
| Distribution evidence | We can identify the relevant contact inside those accounts. | 0–3 |
| Distribution evidence | Our outreach produces replies from the intended market. | 0–3 |
| Distribution evidence | The same message works with more than one similar prospect. | 0–3 |
| Product and retention evidence | Customers can reach the core outcome without extensive founder intervention. | 0–3 |
| Product and retention evidence | Users return to the product or workflow. | 0–3 |
| Product and retention evidence | Customers would be meaningfully affected if access were removed. | 0–3 |
| Product and retention evidence | At least one customer has renewed, expanded, or referred another buyer. | 0–3 |

### Interpreting the score

The maximum score is 60.

- 0–15: Product-first isolation. You are probably building mostly from assumptions. Pause non-essential development and increase customer contact immediately.
- 16–30: Early market contact. You have useful signals, but the customer, buyer, problem, or offer remains unstable. Continue small experiments and avoid major scaling investments.
- 31–45: Emerging commercial pattern. Repeated needs and behaviors are becoming visible. Focus on narrowing the market, improving the offer, and documenting the sales and onboarding motion.
- 46–60: Strong early evidence. You may be approaching a repeatable product-distribution combination. Retention and expansion should now carry more weight than initial excitement.

The score is not a scientific measure of product-market fit. Its purpose is to reveal where confidence exceeds evidence.

## A one-week distribution audit

Before adding another major feature, spend one week examining how your company currently learns from the market.

<Steps>

<Step title="Day 1: Audit your time">

Review the previous four weeks.

Categorize your working hours into:

- Product development
- Infrastructure and maintenance
- Customer discovery
- Sales
- Content
- Partnerships
- Community participation
- Customer support
- Administration

If more than 80% of your time went into building while the product still has few customers, your allocation likely needs correction.

</Step>

<Step title="Day 2: List your assumptions">

Write down your current beliefs about:

- Customer
- User
- Buyer
- Problem
- Urgency
- Existing alternative
- Price
- Sales channel
- Adoption requirements
- Retention

Mark each belief as:

- Assumption
- Customer statement
- Observed behavior
- Commercial evidence

This separation is more important than it seems. Founders often remember repeated assumptions as if they were validated facts.

</Step>

<Step title="Day 3: Review your last ten conversations">

Record:

- Customer type
- Person’s role
- Problem discussed
- Current solution
- Urgency
- Next step
- Commitment made
- Reason the conversation progressed or stopped

If the conversations involve unrelated customer types, you may not be testing one market consistently enough to identify a pattern.

</Step>

<Step title="Day 4: Review feature decisions">

List the last ten features you built or planned.

For each one, record:

- Who requested it?
- How many qualified customers requested it?
- What evidence supported it?
- Did it unblock a purchase?
- Did it improve activation or retention?
- Could the outcome have been delivered manually?

You may discover that a significant portion of the roadmap came from internal imagination rather than customer evidence.

</Step>

<Step title="Day 5: Contact the market">

Choose ten well-qualified prospects.

Do not send a generic product announcement.

Ask about a specific problem:

> We are speaking with examination teams that coordinate question collection and review across Word documents, spreadsheets, and WhatsApp. We are trying to understand where the process creates the most delay or errors. How does your team currently manage it?

The goal is not to close ten deals.

The goal is to create enough contact that your next development decision becomes more informed than the previous one.

</Step>

</Steps>

## What better progress looks like

A technical founder should not stop building.

They should redefine progress.

Progress is not only:

- More features
- Cleaner code
- Better infrastructure
- Faster deployment
- A redesigned interface

Before product-market fit, progress also includes:

- A narrower target market
- A clearer buyer
- A repeated customer problem
- A stronger discovery question
- A message that earns replies
- An objection that appears consistently
- A prospect who introduces the budget owner
- A customer who commits real data or staff time
- A paid pilot
- A user who returns without being chased
- A customer who renews
- A channel that produces another similar opportunity

Some weeks, the most valuable outcome will be discovering that an assumption was wrong.

That can feel like negative progress because it removes certainty.

In reality, it prevents the company from spending more time and money building in the wrong direction.

## The first responsibility is not to scale

Before product-market fit, the founder’s responsibility is not to build a perfect product or a scalable acquisition machine.

It is to discover a viable relationship between:

- A specific customer
- A painful problem
- A valuable outcome
- A workable product
- A believable message
- A reachable channel
- A sustainable commercial model

Technical founders already possess one of the hardest capabilities in a startup: the ability to turn ideas into functioning systems.

The danger is allowing that strength to dominate every decision.

Code can tell you whether the product works according to its specifications.

Only the market can tell you whether the specifications matter.

Build enough to test the value. Speak with customers before certainty feels comfortable. Replace compliments with commitments. Treat distribution as a source of product knowledge rather than an activity reserved for after launch.

A rejected assumption is cheaper than an unwanted product.

## Apply this article

Complete these three actions before beginning your next major feature:

1. Score your startup using the Distribution Readiness Scorecard.
2. Classify your last ten product decisions by the evidence that supported them.
3. Schedule five conversations with people from one narrowly defined customer segment.

Do not use the conversations to demonstrate every feature.

Reconstruct how the customer handled the problem the last time it occurred. Learn who was involved, what it cost, what went wrong, and why the organization has not solved it already.

That evidence will tell you more about what to build next than another week inside the codebase.
