<Abstract>

Teachers don’t want *creative* exam questions. They want questions that match the syllabus they actually taught. This post describes the retrieval-augmented generation design behind ZeroExam AI: hybrid lexical and dense retrieval over syllabus chapters, reciprocal rank fusion, a grounding check on every generated question, and an evaluation plan that separates retrieval quality from generation quality.

</Abstract>

## The problem

A plain LLM asked to “write ten physics questions for grade 9, chapter 3” produces fluent questions, but some of them test concepts that aren’t in that chapter, or in that country’s curriculum at all. For a teacher, one off-syllabus question is enough to stop trusting the tool.

<Term def="Retrieval-augmented generation: retrieve relevant passages first, then condition the model's answer on them.">RAG</Term> is the obvious fix: retrieve the relevant chapter content and make the model generate from it <Cite id="lewis2020" />. The details are where it gets interesting.

### Chunking by structure

Textbooks already have structure: chapters, sections, worked examples, exercises. I chunk along headings first and only split long sections by token count. Each chunk keeps its metadata (`grade`, `subject`, `chapter`, `section`), which lets us filter *before* ranking. Filtering by chapter removes most of the off-syllabus risk on its own.

### Hybrid retrieval

Dense embeddings are good at paraphrase (“speed” and “velocity”). Lexical search like BM25 <Cite id="robertson2009" /> is good at exact terms, formulas and names that embeddings blur together. Exam content has plenty of both, so I run both and fuse the rankings.

### Reciprocal rank fusion

Scores from BM25 and cosine similarity live on different scales, so instead of mixing scores, I mix *ranks*. Reciprocal rank fusion <Cite id="cormack2009" /> scores a document $d$ across the ranked lists $R$ as:

$$
\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in R} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)}
$$

where $k$ is a smoothing constant, commonly $k = 60$. A chunk that appears near the top of either list gets a boost, and one that appears in both does best. It needs no tuning and no score normalisation, which is exactly what you want in an early system.

```python title="fusion.py"
def rrf(rankings: list[list[str]], k: int = 60) -> list[str]:
    scores: dict[str, float] = {}
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)
```

### Context ordering

Models use information at the start and end of a long context more reliably than information in the middle <Cite id="liu2023" />. So I keep the context short (top 6–8 chunks after fusion) and put the strongest chunks first.

<Callout type="insight">

More context is not better context. Fewer, well-ranked chunks gave cleaner questions than stuffing the whole chapter into the prompt.

</Callout>

## Grounding every question

Generation returns structured JSON. Each question must list the chunk IDs that support it:

```json title="question.json"
{
  "question": "A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 20 m/s in 5 s. Find its acceleration.",
  "type": "numerical",
  "difficulty": "easy",
  "answer": "4 m/s²",
  "sources": ["phy-9-ch3-s2-c04"]
}
```

A second, cheaper model call then acts as a verifier: given only the cited chunks, *can this question be answered, and is the answer correct?* Questions that fail are regenerated or dropped. This catches most hallucinated concepts before a teacher ever sees them.

## Evaluation plan

The most common mistake I see in RAG projects is evaluating only the final answer. If the output is bad, you can’t tell whether retrieval or generation failed. So I measure them separately, in the spirit of RAGAS <Cite id="es2023" />.

| Stage | Metric | Definition | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieval | Recall@k | Share of relevant chunks found in the top $k$ | High at $k = 8$ |
| Retrieval | MRR | Mean of $1/\text{rank}$ of the first relevant chunk | Above 0.5 |
| Generation | Faithfulness | Share of questions fully supported by cited chunks | Close to 100% |
| Generation | Syllabus match | Teacher rates question as in-syllabus (yes/no) | Close to 100% |
| Product | Edit rate | Share of questions a teacher edits before printing | Trending down |

Recall at $k$ for a query with relevant set $G$ and retrieved list $\hat{R}_k$:

$$
\mathrm{Recall@}k = \frac{\lvert G \cap \hat{R}_k \rvert}{\lvert G \rvert}
$$

<Callout type="tip" title="Build the gold set with teachers">

Ask two or three teachers to write 30 realistic requests and mark which sections answer them. It takes an afternoon, and it turns “it seems better” into a number.

</Callout>

## Open questions

<Details summary="Things I’m still exploring">

- **Answer checking.** Grading handwritten or typed long answers against a rubric is a much harder problem than generating questions.
- **Urdu-medium content.** Tokenisation and embeddings for Urdu are weaker, which affects both retrieval and generation.
- **Difficulty calibration.** “Hard” according to the model doesn’t always mean hard for students. Real student results are the only reliable signal.

</Details>

<Takeaways>

- Filter by metadata first. It removes most off-syllabus risk cheaply.
- Use hybrid retrieval and fuse ranks, not scores.
- Make every generated item cite its sources, then verify it.
- Evaluate retrieval and generation separately.

</Takeaways>
