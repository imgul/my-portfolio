<TLDR>

In Part 4, we built an initial ideal customer profile to decide which accounts deserve attention. But an ICP remains a hypothesis until conversations with real customers either strengthen or contradict it.

This is where customer discovery begins.

Unfortunately, many founders conduct discovery in a way that produces encouragement rather than evidence. They describe their idea, demonstrate the product, mention its features, and then ask:

“Would you use something like this?”

The prospect says yes.

The founder leaves the conversation feeling validated.

Three months later, the product is ready, but the same prospect does not buy it.

The problem was not dishonesty. Most people are happy to encourage a founder. They can sincerely believe that a product sounds useful while having no intention, authority, urgency, or budget to adopt it.

Useful discovery does not ask customers to predict what they might do.

It reconstructs what they have already done:

- How they handled the problem the last time it occurred
- Who participated in the process
- What tools they used
- Where delays or errors appeared
- What the problem cost
- What they tried previously
- Why their previous attempts failed
- Who owns the problem
- Who can approve a purchase
- What event would make action urgent

The purpose of customer discovery is not to make founders feel confident.

It is to expose the reality in which the product must survive.

</TLDR>

## Customer discovery is not a sales pitch without a price

A founder invites a school administrator to a “discovery call.”

Within five minutes, the founder is sharing a screen.

The remaining conversation becomes a product demonstration:

- Here is the dashboard.
- Here is the AI question generator.
- Here are the user roles.
- Here is the analytics module.
- Here is the approval workflow.
- Here is what we plan to build next.

At the end, the founder asks:

“What do you think?”

The administrator responds:

“It looks impressive. Schools definitely need something like this.”

The founder records the conversation as validation.

But very little was discovered.

The founder did not learn:

- How the school currently prepares examinations
- Whether this process causes a serious problem
- How often the problem occurs
- Whether the administrator owns it
- Whether management wants it solved
- What the school has already tried
- Whether software budget is available
- What adoption would require
- Why the institution might reject the product

The customer reacted to the founder’s solution instead of describing their reality.

That distinction matters.

When prospects see a product too early, they begin discussing the product on your terms. They comment on features, visual design, and hypothetical usefulness. They may unintentionally reorganize their memory of the problem around what you showed them.

Discovery should begin with the customer’s world before introducing yours.

## What customer discovery should produce

A useful discovery process should produce evidence in six areas.

**1. Problem evidence**

You should understand:

- Whether the problem actually occurs
- When it occurred most recently
- How frequently it occurs
- How severe its consequences are
- Who is affected
- Whether it has management attention

**2. Workflow evidence**

You should be able to reconstruct:

- What starts the process
- Which people participate
- What tools are used
- Where information moves
- Where delays or mistakes occur
- Who approves the final outcome

**3. Alternative evidence**

You should learn:

- How the customer handles the problem today
- What works well about the current approach
- What the customer dislikes
- What previous solutions were attempted
- Why the organization continues using the current process

**4. Buying evidence**

You should identify:

- The user
- The problem owner
- The internal champion
- The economic buyer
- The approver
- The technical evaluator
- The potential blocker

**5. Timing evidence**

You should understand:

- What event makes the problem urgent
- Whether a decision timeline exists
- When the relevant budget becomes available
- Whether the customer is actively searching

**6. Commitment evidence**

You should test whether the customer will:

- Schedule another meeting
- Introduce another stakeholder
- Share documents or data
- Allow a workflow observation
- Assign time to a pilot
- Discuss commercial terms
- Pay

A conversation that produces none of these may still be informative. But a sequence of conversations producing only comments and compliments is not commercial progress.

## Why hypothetical questions produce weak evidence

Founders commonly ask questions such as:

- Would you use this?
- Would your team find this helpful?
- Would you pay for this?
- Do schools need an AI examination system?
- Would automation improve your workflow?
- If we built this feature, would you buy it?
- How much would you pay?

These questions force the customer to imagine a future situation.

Future behavior is difficult to predict because the customer is not currently experiencing:

- The implementation burden
- The purchasing process
- The switching cost
- The training effort
- The budget trade-off
- The risk of choosing a new vendor
- The resistance of other employees

Saying yes to a hypothetical question is easy.

Adopting a real product is expensive.

**Weak question**

Would a centralized examination platform save your team time?

The obvious answer is yes.

**Stronger question**

Tell me about the last examination cycle. How did teachers submit their questions, and what happened after submission?

This asks for a real sequence of events.

**Weak question**

Would you pay for a tool that reduces examination preparation time?

The customer has insufficient context and little reason to answer accurately.

**Stronger question**

Have you previously paid for software, outside support, or additional staff to improve this process?

This reveals actual spending behavior.

**Weak question**

Would your teachers use an approval workflow?

**Stronger question**

How are papers currently reviewed and approved, and what happens when a teacher misses a deadline?

The strongest discovery questions usually focus on:

- The most recent occurrence
- Existing behavior
- Existing spending
- Previous attempts
- Specific consequences
- Current responsibilities

Past behavior is not a perfect predictor of future behavior.

It is usually more reliable than hypothetical enthusiasm.

## Reconstruct the last occurrence

One of the most productive discovery prompts is:

> “Tell me about the last time this happened.”

This converts an abstract discussion into a concrete narrative.

Suppose an academic director says:

“Preparing examinations is very difficult.”

Do not immediately ask how software could help.

Ask:

- When did you last prepare an examination cycle?
- What started the process?
- Who was involved?
- What happened first?
- What happened next?
- Which tools were used?
- Where did the process slow down?
- What went wrong?
- Who noticed the problem?
- What happened because of it?
- How was it resolved?
- How long did that take?

The goal is to reconstruct the process like an incident report.

**Example reconstruction**

> The examination controller shares deadlines through WhatsApp
>     ↓
> Teachers prepare questions in separate Word documents
>     ↓
> Documents are sent through WhatsApp or email
>     ↓
> The controller manually checks which teachers submitted
>     ↓
> Subject heads review the questions
>     ↓
> Teachers make corrections in different file versions
>     ↓
> The controller combines and formats the final papers
>     ↓
> The academic director approves printed copies
>     ↓
> The papers are sent for printing

Once the workflow is visible, the founder can investigate its failure points:

- Late submissions
- Missing files
- Duplicate questions
- Inconsistent formatting
- Multiple document versions
- Unclear approval status
- Syllabus gaps
- Difficulty imbalance
- Leakage risk
- Last-minute printing changes

Without this reconstruction, the founder may build a question-generation feature when the customer’s more serious problem is coordination and control.

## Ask for specific examples

Customers often begin with general statements:

“Our process is manual.”

“Teachers do not follow the format.”

“Approvals take too long.”

These statements are useful starting points, but they are not yet sufficient evidence.

Ask for an example.

**Customer statement**

“Teachers submit papers late.”

Follow-up questions

- How many teachers were late during the last cycle?
- How late were they?
- What happened because of the delay?
- Who had to follow up?
- Did the printing date change?
- How was the issue resolved?
- Is this typical or was the last cycle unusual?

**Customer statement**

“There are too many mistakes.”

Follow-up questions

- What type of mistake occurred most recently?
- Who discovered it?
- At what stage was it discovered?
- How much work was required to correct it?
- Did students or parents see the error?
- Was anyone held accountable?
- Has the same type of error happened before?

Specific examples prevent both the customer and the founder from exaggerating the problem.

A serious-sounding complaint may turn out to be rare.

A casually described inconvenience may reveal several days of rework and management escalation.

## Build a workflow map during the conversation

Do not rely only on memory or an unstructured transcript.

Map the workflow while the customer explains it.

| Stage | Person responsible | Tool used | Time required | Failure point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Set examination schedule | Examination controller | Excel, WhatsApp | 2 hours | Changes not tracked |
| Collect questions | Teachers and coordinator | Word, WhatsApp | 3–5 days | Late and missing submissions |
| Review questions | Subject heads | Printed copies, Word | 2–3 days | Multiple file versions |
| Format papers | Examination team | Word | 1–2 days | Manual reformatting |
| Final approval | Academic director | Printed copies | 1 day | Last-minute corrections |
| Send to printing | Examination controller | USB, email | Several hours | Wrong version may be sent |

This map reveals:

- Handoffs
- Delays
- Rework
- Dependency on individuals
- Data duplication
- Process fragmentation
- Opportunities for automation
- Requirements that software must respect

At the end of the explanation, summarize the workflow back to the customer:

“Let me check that I understand. Teachers submit separate Word files through WhatsApp. Your examination team tracks submissions manually, subject heads review printed or digital copies, and your team combines the approved versions before final printing. The largest delays come from late submissions and repeated corrections. Is that accurate?”

This gives the customer an opportunity to correct your model.

That correction is part of discovery.

## Measure the cost of the existing process

A problem becomes commercially interesting when it produces a meaningful cost.

That cost does not need to be purely financial.

It can include:

- Staff time
- Overtime
- Rework
- Delayed operations
- Lost revenue
- Customer complaints
- Compliance exposure
- Security risk
- Management attention
- Employee frustration
- Reputational damage
- Missed opportunities

**Ask about time**

- How many people participate?
- How many hours does each person spend?
- How often does the process occur?
- How much time is spent following up?
- How much additional work occurs when something goes wrong?
- Does the process require overtime?

**Ask about failure**

- How often are errors discovered?
- At what stage are they usually found?
- Who corrects them?
- What other work is delayed?
- Has the process ever missed a deadline?
- Has the issue reached senior management?

**Ask about consequences**

- What happens if the problem is not solved?
- Who complains?
- Who becomes accountable?
- Does it affect customers, students, patients, employees, or regulators?
- Does it create financial loss?
- Does it damage trust?

**A simple cost calculation**

Suppose an examination cycle involves:

- 25 teachers
- 2 hours per teacher for revisions and follow-up
- 3 examination staff
- 30 hours each for collection, consolidation, and formatting
- 4 examination cycles each year

Assume a conservative blended internal labor cost of PKR 600 per hour.

Teacher-related cost:

25 teachers × 2 hours × PKR 600 × 4 cycles = PKR 120,000 annually

Examination-team cost:

3 employees × 30 hours × PKR 600 × 4 cycles = PKR 216,000 annually

Estimated annual staff-time cost:

PKR 120,000 + PKR 216,000 = PKR 336,000 annually

This estimate does not include:

- Printing mistakes
- Management time
- Security failures
- Student complaints
- Delayed results
- Opportunity cost

The purpose is not to manufacture an inflated return-on-investment calculation. It is to understand whether the problem is commercially meaningful.

If the problem costs approximately PKR 336,000 per year, a PKR 1 million annual product may be difficult to justify on labor savings alone.

The product would need to create additional value through risk reduction, quality improvement, capacity, or another measurable outcome.

Customer discovery should expose this before pricing and product scope are finalized.

## Understand the current alternative before criticizing it

Founders often assume that spreadsheets, paper, email, or WhatsApp are obviously inferior to specialized software.

Customers may see them differently.

A spreadsheet may be:

- Familiar
- Flexible
- Cheap
- Easy to modify
- Owned by the team
- Compatible with existing habits
- Usable without training

WhatsApp may be:

- Already installed
- Frequently checked
- Easy for teachers to use
- Reliable under limited connectivity
- Connected to trusted personal relationships

Paper may provide:

- Easy review
- Physical control
- Familiar signatures
- A sense of confidentiality
- Compatibility with existing approval practices

The current process may be inefficient while still possessing advantages your product must respect.

Ask:

- What works well about the current process?
- Why has the organization continued using it?
- Which part would employees resist changing?
- What would be lost if the current system disappeared?
- Who has become skilled at managing it?
- Does the process handle unusual cases better than existing software?
- Which previous replacement attempts failed?

**The “good enough” threshold**

Your product is not competing with an ideal solution.

It is competing with:

The current process plus the customer’s tolerance for its problems.

If the current process is painful but familiar, the product needs to create enough incremental value to justify:

- Migration
- Training
- Purchase approval
- Workflow change
- Vendor risk
- Implementation time

Discovery should identify the threshold at which the customer stops tolerating the workaround.

## Investigate previous attempts to solve the problem

A customer who has already attempted to solve a problem provides stronger evidence than one who merely complains about it.

Ask:

- Have you tried to improve this process before?
- What did you try?
- Who initiated the effort?
- Was money allocated?
- What happened?
- Why did it fail?
- What did employees dislike?
- What would need to be different this time?

Previous attempts reveal:

- The seriousness of the problem
- Internal ownership
- Budget history
- Technical constraints
- Change resistance
- Procurement behavior
- Failed assumptions
- Incumbent competitors

**Example**

A school may have purchased an examination module inside a larger school-management system but stopped using it because:

- Teachers found data entry too slow.
- The module required constant internet access.
- Existing Word formats could not be imported.
- The approval process did not match school operations.
- Training was insufficient.
- Only one administrator understood the system.
- Vendor support was slow.
- Reports could not be customized.

This is highly valuable evidence.

It does not prove that the school wants your product. It tells you what your product and implementation process must overcome.

A failed competitor deployment may indicate opportunity.

It may also indicate that adoption is structurally difficult.

## Distinguish inconvenience from urgency

A problem can be real without being important.

Many founders discover legitimate inefficiencies and assume that customers will pay to remove them.

But customers tolerate enormous amounts of inefficiency when:

- The cost is distributed across employees.
- The process occurs infrequently.
- No senior person owns the outcome.
- The organization has more urgent priorities.
- The workaround is familiar.
- Failure creates no serious consequence.
- Budget is unavailable.
- Solving the problem requires too much change.

Ask questions that reveal priority:

- What happens if you do nothing?
- Who is responsible for improving this?
- Is there a deadline?
- Has management discussed it?
- Is money allocated?
- Which other initiatives compete with it?
- What would cause this project to be delayed?
- Why solve it now instead of next year?

**Signs of inconvenience**

- The customer agrees that the process is inefficient.
- No one owns the improvement.
- No decision timeline exists.
- The customer has never tried to solve it.
- The consequences are vague.
- Meetings repeatedly end without a next step.

**Signs of urgency**

- A recent failure occurred.
- A deadline is approaching.
- Leadership assigned responsibility.
- The organization is evaluating alternatives.
- Budget planning is underway.
- A current system is being replaced.
- Staff time has been allocated.
- The customer introduces the buyer.
- A pilot date is discussed.

Urgency does not mean the customer sounds emotional.

It means the organization is moving.

## Identify the buying system

A product may receive enthusiastic responses from users while never reaching a buyer.

Customer discovery must reveal how organizational decisions are made.

**The user**

Who interacts with the product?

For an examination platform:

- Teachers
- Examination coordinators
- Academic administrators

**The problem owner**

Who becomes accountable when the process fails?

Possible roles:

- Examination controller
- Academic director
- Principal

**The champion**

Who wants the project to succeed and can move it internally?

A real champion may:

- Arrange the next meeting
- Explain internal politics
- Share documents
- Involve colleagues
- Defend the project
- Coordinate a pilot

**The economic buyer**

Who controls the relevant budget?

Possible roles:

- School owner
- Principal
- Finance director
- Head-office administrator

**The approver**

Whose authorization is required?

This might be:

- The owner
- A board
- A central administration
- A procurement committee

**The evaluator**

Who assesses technical, legal, security, or operational feasibility?

**The blocker**

Who or what can stop the decision?

Ask:

- Who is most affected by this problem?
- Who is accountable when it goes wrong?
- Who would need to support a pilot?
- Who controls the budget?
- Who signs the agreement?
- Who evaluates the technology?
- Who might resist the change?
- How was a similar purchase approved previously?

The final question is especially useful because it asks about real purchasing behavior rather than a hypothetical process.

## Discuss budget without asking “What would you pay?”

Founders often avoid budget questions because they fear making the conversation feel like a sales call.

But a problem without a purchasing path is not a commercial opportunity.

The solution is not to ask a vague question such as:

“How much would you pay for this?”

The customer may not know. They may anchor low, avoid answering, or provide a number unrelated to internal procurement.

Instead, investigate the budget context.

**Ask about existing spending**

- Are you currently paying for software related to this workflow?
- Have you previously hired outside support?
- How was the previous solution funded?
- Which department owns this budget?
- Is the cost treated as software, operations, academic services, or another category?

**Ask about approval thresholds**

- What level of spending can the principal approve?
- When does owner or board approval become necessary?
- Would a pilot and annual contract follow different approval processes?
- Is vendor registration required?

**Ask about budget timing**

- When are annual budgets planned?
- Can an urgent purchase happen outside the budget cycle?
- Are payments made monthly, annually, or by project?
- What are the normal payment terms?

**Ask about comparable purchases**

“When the school last purchased academic or administrative software, how did the decision work?”

This reveals:

- Internal stakeholders
- Budget ownership
- Evaluation criteria
- Procurement timing
- Trust requirements
- Contract expectations

For early-stage sales, budget discovery should determine whether the problem exists inside a fundable category.

## Record the customer’s exact language

Founders often translate customer statements into startup terminology.

A school administrator says:

“We spend two days chasing teachers for papers.”

The founder records:

“Customer needs workflow automation.”

The translation loses valuable language.

The customer’s wording can improve:

- Positioning
- Outreach
- Website copy
- Content
- Sales demonstrations
- Product labels
- Objection handling

Record exact phrases such as:

- “We have no idea which paper is final.”
- “Every teacher sends a different format.”
- “The principal asks us why printing is late.”
- “We cannot force teachers to learn another complicated system.”
- “The software we tried did not work without internet.”
- “Our team is comfortable with Word.”
- “We do not want to replace the entire school system.”

Compare two marketing messages.

**Founder language**

AI-powered assessment workflow automation for modern educational institutions.

**Customer language**

Stop chasing teachers, combining different Word files, and guessing which question paper is final.

The second message is more specific because it reflects lived experience.

Do not copy customer language mechanically. Use it to understand how the market recognizes and describes the problem.

## Separate notes from interpretation

After each conversation, maintain two layers.

**Layer 1: What the customer said or did**

Examples:

- The examination controller said it takes two days to combine final papers.
- The school uses WhatsApp for teacher submissions.
- The principal approves purchases above PKR 100,000.
- The customer introduced the academic director.
- The school declined to share sample documents.
- The buyer requested a proposal before discussing a pilot.

**Layer 2: Your interpretation**

Examples:

- Manual consolidation may be a high-value automation opportunity.
- The controller appears to be a possible champion.
- Buyer access may be required earlier.
- Data confidentiality may be a major adoption concern.
- The customer may be collecting proposals without urgent intent.

Keeping the layers separate reduces confirmation bias.

Interpretations can change as more evidence appears.

Observed statements and actions should remain intact.

## How to avoid confirmation bias

Founders are not neutral interviewers. They want the idea to work.

That desire influences:

- Which questions they ask
- Which answers they remember
- Which objections they dismiss
- Which customers they pursue
- Which signals they call validation

Use the following safeguards.

**1. Ask disconfirming questions**

Do not ask only what supports the product.

Ask:

- Why is this not a serious problem?
- Why might the current process be good enough?
- What would prevent you from adopting this?
- What other priority is more important?
- Why did the previous software fail?
- What would make a pilot unsuccessful?
- Under what circumstances would you never purchase this?

**2. Interview lost or uninterested prospects**

Founders prefer people who respond positively.

Negative prospects may reveal:

- Weak pain
- Poor timing
- Buyer mismatch
- Trust barriers
- Alternative strength
- Unacceptable adoption cost

**3. Require behavioral evidence**

Do not mark an assumption validated because three customers agreed with it.

Look for:

- Recent examples
- Existing spending
- Previous attempts
- Internal introductions
- Shared data
- Pilot participation
- Payment

**4. Write predictions before the call**

Before each conversation, record:

- What problem you expect
- What tools you expect
- Who you think the buyer is
- What trigger you expect
- What objection you expect

After the call, compare prediction with reality.

This exposes how often you reinterpret results to fit the original idea.

**5. Review patterns across similar accounts**

One conversation produces an anecdote.

Repeated behavior across similar customers produces a market signal.

## How many conversations are enough?

There is no universal number.

Five conversations may expose an obvious flaw. Fifty conversations may still produce confusion if they involve unrelated segments and poorly structured questions.

Before drawing conclusions, look for pattern quality.

**Weak pattern**

- Ten conversations across five industries
- Different roles in every call
- Mostly hypothetical questions
- No workflow observations
- No meaningful next steps

**Stronger pattern**

- Ten conversations with the same account type
- Similar operational roles
- Repeated workflow
- Recent examples
- Similar consequences
- Comparable buying process
- Several customers making stronger commitments

As an early operating rule:

- Use the first 5 conversations to improve your questions.
- Use approximately 10 qualified conversations to identify early patterns.
- Use 20 to 30 similar-account conversations to evaluate whether those patterns persist.
- Require commercial or operational commitments before treating the market as validated.

The number is less important than the comparability of the evidence.

## What to do when customers contradict one another

Contradiction is normal.

One school may say offline access is mandatory.

Another may use cloud software comfortably.

One academic director may care about question quality.

Another may care primarily about deadline tracking.

One owner may approve software quickly.

Another may require a committee and annual budget.

Do not average contradictory feedback into a bloated product.

Investigate the cause.

**Check whether the customers belong to different segments**

Differences may correlate with:

- Geography
- Size
- Fee level
- Number of campuses
- Curriculum
- Technology maturity
- Ownership
- Buyer role
- Existing systems

**Check whether they perform different workflows**

Two institutions in the same industry may solve different operational problems.

**Check whether one request is a preference and another is a requirement**

A customer may prefer a mobile application but still adopt without one.

A regulatory restriction may make a deployment model non-negotiable.

**Check the strength of the evidence**

A prospect may claim offline access is essential.

A paying pilot customer may use the cloud product successfully.

Observed adoption should carry more weight than hypothetical requirements.

Contradictions often reveal that the ICP is still too broad.

Do not ask:

“Which customer is correct?”

Ask:

“What characteristic explains why these customers need different things?”

## The 30-minute customer discovery interview

A structured interview keeps the conversation focused without turning it into an interrogation.

**Minutes 0–3: Set expectations**

Use a simple opening:

“Thank you for speaking with me. I’m trying to understand how examination teams currently prepare, review, and approve assessment material. I’m less interested in pitching a product today and more interested in how the process works in practice. I’ll ask about the last examination cycle and may take notes. Is that okay?”

This reduces pressure and encourages specificity.

**Minutes 3–8: Establish context**

Ask:

- What is your role?
- Which parts of the examination process are you responsible for?
- How large is the institution?
- How many teachers, subjects, grades, or campuses are involved?
- How often does the process occur?

Do not spend half the interview collecting general background.

Gather only enough context to interpret the workflow.

**Minutes 8–18: Reconstruct the last occurrence**

Ask:

- Tell me about the last examination cycle.
- What started the process?
- What happened first?
- What happened next?
- Who participated?
- Which tools did you use?
- Where did delays or errors occur?
- What happened because of them?
- How did the team resolve them?

This is the most important part of the interview.

**Minutes 18–23: Explore alternatives and attempted solutions**

Ask:

- What works well about the current process?
- What have you tried to improve?
- Have you used software for this?
- Why did the previous approach succeed or fail?
- Why does the current process remain in place?

**Minutes 23–27: Understand priority and purchasing**

Ask:

- Is improving this process currently a priority?
- What event would make it urgent?
- Who owns the problem?
- Who would evaluate a solution?
- Who would approve a pilot or purchase?
- How was a similar purchase handled previously?

**Minutes 27–30: Request a next step**

Depending on the evidence, ask for:

- A workflow walkthrough
- Sample documents
- Introduction to another stakeholder
- A second discussion with the buyer
- A pilot-scoping session

Example:

“The repeated version changes and approval delays appear to be the most costly part of the process. The next useful step would be a 45-minute workflow session with you and the examination controller using one recent paper as an example. That would let us determine whether a small pilot is practical. Would that be useful?”

The next step should match the strength of the evidence.

Do not force a pilot when the problem remains unclear.

## Customer discovery interview script

Use this as a flexible guide, not a questionnaire that must be read word for word.

**Context**

- What is your role?
- Which part of this process do you own?
- How many people participate?
- How frequently does the process occur?

**Recent behavior**

- Tell me about the last time you handled it.
- What started the process?
- What happened first?
- What happened next?
- Which tools did you use?
- How long did each stage take?

**Problems and consequences**

- Where did the process slow down?
- What went wrong?
- Who was affected?
- What did the team do to recover?
- How much time or money did that require?
- What happens if the problem remains unsolved?

**Alternatives**

- What works well about the current process?
- What do you dislike?
- Have you tried another approach?
- Why did that attempt succeed or fail?
- Why have you kept the current solution?

**Priority and trigger**

- Is anyone responsible for improving this?
- Is there a deadline or upcoming event?
- Why would you solve this now?
- What could cause the organization to delay it?

**Buying system**

- Who experiences the problem most directly?
- Who would coordinate an evaluation?
- Who controls the budget?
- Who approves the purchase?
- Who might oppose it?
- How was the last similar purchase made?

**Commitment**

- Can you introduce me to the person responsible for this stage?
- Can we review a recent workflow or document?
- Would your team consider a limited pilot?
- What would need to be true for that pilot to proceed?

## Score the quality of each discovery conversation

After each call, score it across five dimensions.

Use a scale from 0 to 3:

- 0: No evidence
- 1: Weak evidence
- 2: Moderate evidence
- 3: Strong evidence

| Dimension | Score |
| --- | --- |
| Recent problem described | /3 |
| Workflow reconstructed | /3 |
| Cost or consequence identified | /3 |
| Buying system identified | /3 |
| Meaningful next commitment | /3 |
| **Total** | **/15** |

**Interpretation**

- 0–4: Low-value conversation. The discussion remained general or hypothetical.
- 5–8: Useful discovery. Some workflow and problem evidence appeared, but key areas remain unclear.
- 9–12: Strong discovery. The customer described real behavior, consequences, and organizational context.
- 13–15: Commercially meaningful discovery. The problem is concrete, stakeholders are visible, and the customer made a meaningful commitment.

Do not evaluate the interviewer only by how much the prospect liked the conversation.

Evaluate whether useful evidence emerged.

## Turn discovery into product decisions

Discovery should eventually affect the product.

But founders should not convert every statement into a feature.

Classify findings into five categories.

**1. Repeated problem**

Example:

Examination controllers repeatedly lose track of which document version has received final approval.

Possible response:

Prioritize version control and approval status.

**2. Environmental constraint**

Example:

Several qualified schools experience inconsistent connectivity.

Possible response:

Investigate resilient synchronization, low-bandwidth operation, or offline support.

This may represent a market requirement rather than a feature preference.

**3. Adoption requirement**

Example:

Schools will only test the system if they can import existing Word or spreadsheet data.

Possible response:

Build a limited import path before broader functionality.

**4. Individual preference**

Example:

One principal wants a custom executive dashboard with specific colors.

Possible response:

Record it, but do not prioritize without repeated evidence or commercial impact.

**5. Adjacent-market request**

Example:

A school asks for fee collection, attendance, payroll, and transportation management.

Possible response:

Exclude these from the examination-product roadmap unless the company intentionally changes strategy.

A useful product decision asks:

- How many qualified accounts revealed this?
- Did they reveal it independently?
- Does it block adoption?
- Does it affect the core outcome?
- Is it specific to the beachhead?
- Is it supported by behavioral or commercial evidence?
- Can it be tested manually first?

## Turn discovery into better positioning

Repeated customer language should improve how you describe the product.

Suppose discovery produces these statements:

- “We keep following up with teachers.”
- “Everyone submits a different Word format.”
- “We do not know which file is final.”
- “Corrections continue until the printing deadline.”
- “The software we tried wanted us to change the whole school system.”

A stronger positioning statement might become:

> Help your examination team collect, review, approve, and finalize question papers without chasing teachers across WhatsApp or replacing your existing school system.

This statement communicates:

- The operational workflow
- The existing pain
- The desired outcome
- A major adoption concern

This is stronger than:

> An AI-powered assessment ecosystem for the future of education.

Discovery improves positioning by replacing founder abstraction with customer recognition.

## When to show the product

Customer discovery does not mean hiding the product forever.

Show it after you understand enough context to make the demonstration relevant.

A practical sequence is:

1. Ask about the customer’s role.
2. Reconstruct the current workflow.
3. Identify the important problem.
4. Understand previous attempts.
5. Clarify urgency and stakeholders.
6. Summarize what you heard.
7. Ask permission to show the relevant workflow.
8. Demonstrate only what connects to the problem.
9. Observe reactions and objections.
10. Request a concrete next step.

For example:

“You mentioned that your team loses track of document versions after subject-head reviews. I can show you the approval workflow we are testing. It takes about five minutes. Would that be useful?”

The demonstration now has context.

The customer can compare it against real behavior rather than evaluating an abstract feature.

## Common customer discovery failure modes

**Failure 1: Pitching too early**

The founder dominates the conversation and learns little about the customer’s workflow.

Correction

Do not demonstrate until you can summarize the customer’s current process and primary problem accurately.

**Failure 2: Interviewing only friendly contacts**

Friends and former clients may protect the founder from difficult feedback.

Correction

Include unfamiliar prospects, lost opportunities, and people with no social reason to encourage you.

**Failure 3: Asking leading questions**

“Wouldn’t automation save your team significant time?”

The desired answer is obvious.

Correction

Ask:

“Which part of the process consumes the most time?”

**Failure 4: Accepting vague answers**

“The process is difficult.”

Correction

Ask:

“Tell me what happened during the last cycle.”

**Failure 5: Talking only to users**

Users can reveal workflow problems but may not understand budget and approval.

Correction

Interview users, problem owners, champions, and buyers separately.

**Failure 6: Treating every request as a requirement**

One customer asks for a mobile application, and the roadmap changes.

Correction

Look for repeated, independently revealed requirements attached to real commitments.

**Failure 7: Failing to ask for a next step**

The conversation ends pleasantly but produces no behavioral evidence.

Correction

Ask for a commitment appropriate to the stage.

**Failure 8: Recording summaries instead of evidence**

“Customer liked the product.”

Correction

Record what they said, what they did, what they shared, and what they agreed to do next.

**Failure 9: Over-interviewing without selling**

The founder conducts 50 interviews but never asks anyone to test or pay.

Correction

Once the problem pattern becomes credible, convert discovery into pilot and pricing conversations.

**Failure 10: Interviewing unrelated segments**

Every conversation reveals a different workflow.

Correction

Conduct comparable interviews within the selected ICP before expanding.

## A 14-day customer discovery sprint

This sprint is designed to produce ten qualified conversations and at least three stronger commitments.

<Steps>

<Step title="Days 1 and 2: Prepare">

Review ICP v0.1.
Select one customer segment.
Define the problem hypothesis.
Create the interview guide.
Prepare the discovery evidence record.
List the assumptions you want to test.
Define what counts as a qualified conversation.

**Qualified-conversation criteria**

A conversation qualifies when:

- The account reasonably matches the ICP.
- The interviewee participates in or owns the workflow.
- A recent occurrence can be discussed.
- At least 20 minutes are available.
- The conversation is not limited to a generic product reaction.

</Step>

<Step title="Days 3 and 4: Recruit participants">

Build a list of 30 to 50 accounts.

Use:

- Warm introductions
- Former clients
- Professional communities
- LinkedIn outreach
- Email
- Industry associations
- Local networks
- Relevant consultants and implementation providers

Use a research-oriented message:

I’m studying how private schools coordinate question collection, review, and final approval before examination cycles. I’m particularly interested in where Word documents, spreadsheets, and WhatsApp create delays or rework. I’m not asking you to purchase anything during the call. Would you be open to a 20-minute conversation about the last examination cycle?

Avoid claiming that you are “just conducting research” if you also intend to evaluate commercial fit. Be transparent when the conversation moves toward a product or pilot.

</Step>

<Step title="Days 5 to 10: Conduct ten interviews">

For every interview:

- Reconstruct the last occurrence.
- Map the workflow.
- Identify the cost.
- Investigate alternatives.
- Understand the buying system.
- Ask about triggers.
- Record exact language.
- Request a next step where appropriate.

Complete the evidence record immediately after the call.

</Step>

<Step title="Days 8 to 11: Request stronger commitments">

For qualified accounts, ask for:

- Three workflow walkthroughs
- Three sets of example documents or anonymized data
- Three internal introductions
- Two pilot-scoping discussions

These targets can overlap.

You are testing whether expressed interest converts into behavior.

</Step>

<Step title="Day 12: Analyze patterns">

Create a table:

| Finding | Number of accounts | Evidence strength | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Late teacher submissions | 8/10 | Statements and workflow examples | Problem is repeated |
| Multiple Word versions | 7/10 | Observed documents | Approval workflow may matter |
| Offline access required | 3/10 | Statements only | Investigate by account type |
| Full ERP requested | 2/10 | Requests only | Likely adjacent-market pull |
| Upcoming exam creates urgency | 6/10 | Next-step behavior | Strong trigger |
| Buyer is school owner | 7/10 | Purchasing history | Outreach and sales must involve owner |

</Step>

<Step title="Day 13: Make decisions">

Decide what should change in:

- ICP
- Problem statement
- Target role
- Outreach message
- Product priority
- Pilot design
- Pricing hypothesis
- Adoption requirements

Choose one or two meaningful changes.

Do not rebuild the entire strategy from ten interviews.

</Step>

<Step title="Day 14: Move into commercial testing">

Select the strongest accounts and propose:

- A workflow assessment
- A paid pilot
- A limited implementation
- A commercial proof of concept

Discovery that never progresses toward commercial evidence becomes another form of avoidance.

</Step>

</Steps>

## What good discovery feels like

Good customer discovery does not always make the founder feel encouraged.

It may reveal:

- The problem is less serious than expected.
- The intended user cannot influence the purchase.
- The customer needs implementation rather than software alone.
- The market requires offline functionality.
- Existing workarounds are difficult to replace.
- The price customers can support is too low.
- The sales cycle exceeds available runway.
- The original feature is less valuable than an adjacent workflow.
- The selected ICP contains several different subsegments.

This can feel like failure because it reduces confidence.

In reality, it improves decision quality.

The most expensive customer discovery is the discovery performed after the product is fully built.

Early discovery creates the option to:

- Narrow the market
- Change the buyer
- Reframe the problem
- Adjust the product
- Redesign the offer
- Abandon a weak opportunity
- Preserve runway

The purpose is not to prove that your idea is right.

It is to discover what would need to be true for the business to work, then test whether those conditions exist.

## From compliments to commitments

The most useful discovery conversations move through a sequence.

<Flow steps={[
  'The customer acknowledges the problem',
  'Describes a recent occurrence',
  'Explains the current workflow',
  'Quantifies the consequence',
  'Reveals previous attempts',
  'Identifies the stakeholders',
  'Introduces another decision-maker',
  'Shares data or documents',
  'Commits staff time',
  'Discusses a pilot',
  'Accepts commercial terms'
]} />

Not every conversation must reach the bottom.

But your discovery system should move qualified opportunities toward increasingly costly commitments.

A customer who says the problem is serious but refuses to spend 30 minutes reviewing the workflow is providing evidence.

A prospect who praises the product but will not introduce the buyer is providing evidence.

A buyer who agrees to a pilot but refuses to assign staff or define success is providing evidence.

Customer behavior completes the sentence that customer words begin.

## Apply this article

During the next 14 days:

1. Select one customer profile from ICP v0.1.
2. Recruit ten people involved in the same workflow.
3. Ask about the most recent occurrence rather than hypothetical future behavior.
4. Map each workflow from trigger to outcome.
5. Estimate the cost in time, money, risk, or delay.
6. Investigate existing alternatives and previous attempts.
7. Identify the user, problem owner, champion, buyer, approver, and blocker.
8. Record exact customer language.
9. Ask every qualified prospect for an appropriate next commitment.
10. Make one product and one distribution decision based on repeated evidence.

Do not summarize the sprint by saying:

“Customers liked the idea.”

Summarize it in observable terms:

“Seven of ten schools used Word and WhatsApp to coordinate examination papers. Six described version-control problems during their last cycle. Four introduced an academic decision-maker, three shared sample documents, and two agreed to scope a paid pilot before their next examination cycle.”

That is evidence.
